The data used for the project was originally based on the manual transcription of 36 historical timetable books from Berlin public transport authority (BVG), held in Berlin Archive (BVG Archive) and the Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm Centre Library, which initially were meant to serve transportation operations and management and then digitized by Noah Kim-Baumann. The data was organized as three mutually related csv files which were lines.csv, stations.csv and line_stops.csv. Data covered 13 snapshots from 1946 to 1989, thus can support diachronic comparisons while can barely reflect short-term changes between two sample years. Meanwhile, visibilities of different modes of transportation also vary in dataset. Compared to other modes (e.g. buses and trams), U-Bahn and S-Bahn records are more complete in station coverage. In earlier periods, the former modes in timetable books only include selected documentations considered important, whereby the selection criteria vary across years and regions, meaning that the dataset only reflects decisions about what aspects of the transport network were considered worth recording. As to specific data information, the “frequency” data refers only to 7:30 on workdays. Daily, weekly, and seasonal variations are not recorded. The coordinates of some buses and trams are still approximately determined based on historical intersection information, so their spatial locations are also subject to some uncertainty. Absence or missing data can also refer to different meanings. For example, if a station is missing from our 1961 data, it could mean that it was genuinely closed, that it still existed but was not included in the dataset, or that the relevant change happened between two of our sampled years.
All the limitations above mean that the dataset mainly describes the transport network and planned services recorded by the official government, rather than practical and actual experiences of passengers. Information such as passengers' age, gender, class, ethnicity, disability status, income, and migration background is not present in the data. Hence we cannot determine how the transport network structure affects the social groups, which further means it is impossible to analyze social effects such as how transportation of Berlin during the Cold War influenced social economy, culture etc. without other datasets and convincing related research.
Based on the limitations, we chose Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf as our research subjects to explore the comparison of the two district regions during restructuring and division of Cold War Berlin by modelling temporal public transport systems. It is also worth noticing that our research expectations themselves may lead to results closer to the anticipated outcome. Therefore, we place greater emphasis on the data processing and modeling process. This study measures the cross-regional direct reachability between Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf by spatial screening and route-station connection, and compares the public transport connectivity between the two regions in different years by combining the distribution of route frequency intervals. While the “connectivity” concept and its scope was defined by the research team. Despite the fact that we referenced Misha's definition of the multidimensional concept of connectivity, given the limitations of database visibility, we still mainly focused our research on the reachability, frequency, and coverage of kilometres of lines as standards to determine the connectivity. We used GeoPandas and polygon tools to create heatmaps and histograms for visualization. We also excluded Kreuzberg or Wilmersdorf's own data from color value comparisons to highlight external connections. Furthermore, we used a uniform color scale across all years to improve comparability between different maps and enhance the reading experience. However, the accuracy of geolocation latitude and longitude data still has a subtle impact.
