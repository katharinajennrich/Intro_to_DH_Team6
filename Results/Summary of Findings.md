# Summary of Findings
## Thesis-Related Findings

All four analyses support (with varying confidence) our thesis that Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf were comparably well-connected during the partition of Berlin. The coverage analysis supports the two districts having similar station coverage throughout the surveyed years. This conclusion holds up on two levels of sensitivity, though the irregular recording of stations in early years still limits its confidence. The frequency histograms indicate comparable frequency of departures in both districts, and fluctuations with time that are consistent between the two districts. As evidence the histograms are good, though limited by the small number of sample years. The heat maps do not support major differences in other areas of Berlin accessible from the two districts. These maps are a fairly ambiguous measure, since they rely on visual interpretation, so they can mainly be said to disprove the negative, rather than prove our thesis. The pie charts of transit modes also show similar changes occurring across both districts, however they cannot be interpreted situationally. Thus, this analysis supports the two districts’ public transit being comparable, but not necessarily comparable in connectivity.

A number of analyses also support an increase in connectivity after 1951. This conclusion is much more tentative due to the major possible impact the sporadic recording of stations may have had on this trend. However, since the frequency analysis also pointed towards an improvement in connectivity with time, we have decided to include this trend, cautioning that it is with low confidence. The coverage analysis indicates an increase in station coverage after 1951, which shows a strong trend, but with the major limitation of possible missing data. The range analysis suggests an increase in stations in bordering/closeby districts connected to Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf after 1951, though with the same major limitation of the stations records. The frequency histograms show a slight increase in lines with more frequent departures in later years, and are limited mainly by the small number of sample years. The pie charts showing transit modes cannot be used to support this part of the thesis.

## Detailed Results and Interpretations

### Coverage Analysis:

This analysis compares the amount of area within a certain radius of a public transit station with the total area of the district. It is intended to provide a measure of how easy it was for a person in that district to access public transit. The maps show the radii overlaid on the district, while the plots show the percentage coverage over the five surveyed years, with Kreuzberg in blue and Wilmersdorf in orange. We performed this analysis with three radii measures (250, 500, 1000 meters) which correspond to approximately three, six and twelve minutes walking distance. We chose this visualization in part because, as a percentage measurement, it makes the districts directly comparable despite their difference in area. A heat map analysis of even smaller sections within each district and the stations within them would have provided an alternative analysis of accessibility of stations within each district.

For both Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf, all of the district was within 1000 meters of a transit station in all surveyed years. This indicates that people had good access to public transit throughout the partition period, but does not provide us any information with which we can compare the districts. The 500 and 250 meter radii analysis provide a more sensitive analysis of the same question. The 500 meter analysis shows a noticeable increase in coverage from about 90% in both districts in 1951, to in the high 90% range for both districts consistently in all later years. This suggests that both districts had better access to public transit after 1951. The 250 meter analysis allows for even more sensitive results, showing not just an increase after 1951 in both districts, but also a slight decrease in 1967. The 250 meter analysis also shows slight differences in coverage between the two districts, with Kreuzberg showing slightly better coverage until 1984, where its coverage drops in comparison to both Wilmersdorf in 1984, and itself in the previous survey year 1976. This exchange in slightly better coverage indicates that neither district can be said to have consistently better access to public transit stations; this analysis suggests comparable connectivity between the two districts.

This analysis is limited in that it flattens a lived-in area into a percentage of coverage, which does not take into account other factors that might require more coverage in some areas over others. It should thus be taken into account that 90% coverage could correspond to a different lived experience depending on which 10% of the district is not covered. It is also limited, as are all of our analyses, by the sporadic coverage of stations in the early years. Since bus and tram stops were recorded based on importance, it may be that the increase in coverage seen after 1951 is a result of stricter standards for importance than later years. These limitations have a significant impact on our thesis of an improvement in connectivity after 1951. However, since the impact of missing data is impossible to quantify, we chose to include this pattern while cautioning its direct reliance on possible flawed data.


### Frequency Histogram Analysis:

This analysis demonstrates the frequency with which lines run in each district in each year. It is intended to demonstrate how frequently a person would have been able to take public transit, with more frequent connections being interpreted as better connectivity. The histograms show the minutes between departures on the x-axis, and the number of lines for which said departure frequency applies on the y-axis. The histogram uses boxes of five minutes, with the exception of the box that demonstrates the zero values, which is coded to take any values between 0 and 0.5. (The first blue box then also takes values 0.5-5, but since the frequency is given in integers this includes all non-zero values). In practice, this allows us to isolate all zero values, which we interpreted as likely being missing data. We interpreted this due to their appearance only on the S-Bahn lines which we might expect to have been more sporadic since they in part connected Berlin with more farlying areas, and so were not purely intra-city transport. We considered performing this analysis as a simple mean of frequency for each year, but expanded to a histogram format as it would provide additional context. 

From the histograms we see that both districts are subject to similar changes in line frequency. Evident is the increase in total number of lines in 1961 and 1967, which also results in a spike in transit running every 10-15 minutes. Both districts also saw a subsequent decrease in total number of lines in 1976 and 1981. This might indicate the greatest number of options for travellers in 1976 and 1981, though the histogram cannot tell us where the lines in question terminate, so cannot rule out highly similar connections. There is also a leftward shift visible with time, which corresponds to an increase in more frequent connections, and a decrease in less frequent connections. This is best demonstrated by focussing on the 0.5-5 minute box, which has no lines in either district until 1967, and increases to two lines in both by 1981. This leftward shift indicates that travellers had more frequent access to public transit in the later years than in 1951, indicating better connectivity in both districts. The only highly evident difference between the two districts is the Kreuzberg line running at a frequency of every 115-120 minutes in 1951, which is a complete outlier from the rest of the years in Kreuzberg as well as Wilmersdorf. It is thus not interpreted as being relevant to the overall trends. 

This analysis is limited mainly by data and context concerns: frequency tells us how often public transit runs, but not how many people it can move. Thus, we cannot neatly interpret an improvement in frequency as a larger number of people served. We also did not connect these frequency diagrams with which transit mode each line runs as, which would have provided some extra context for how a traveller would have experienced an improvement in frequency. The small number of sample years is also a concern, though they do span a number of years. Despite these limitations, the trend of comparability between the districts as well as the improvement in frequency with time holds up, and can be claimed with moderate confidence.

### Range (Heat Map) Analysis:

This analysis shows what other parts of Berlin Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf were connected to via public transit. It is intended to indicate where and how far a person would have been able to travel using a single transit line. The maps assign each district a shade of blue based on how many stations in that district have a direct connection to either Kreuzberg or Wilmersdorf. The maps have a fixed scale across both districts and all years in order to facilitate comparison, and Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf themselves have been removed, since we are not interested in intra-district connectivity in this analysis. Important to note is that the district borders are modern, so should not be considered as anything but regions of Berlin that have been delineated for representational purposes. Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf are also given by their current borders, but these (sub)districts have not changed their borders since 1951. This analysis could be performed similarly as a network analysis, which would provide the additional dimension of travel from other districts into the surveyed district. 

This analysis does not show major differences between districts, and due to its visual character is challenging to use for any subtle differences. Both districts show a slight increase in medium-blue shaded districts after 1951, which would indicate more stations in those districts. Since the districts experiencing this darkening are located fairly close to Kreuzberg and Wilmersdorf respectively, this can be interpreted as an improvement in connectivity to neighboring and near-neighboring districts after 1951. There is also a clear lack of any connected stations in the eastern side of Berlin in 1976 and 1984 from either district, reflecting the partition of Berlin and the subsequent enforcing of its border. However, there is evident indication of stations connected to Kreuzberg in East Berlin in 1967, which is notable as it is an increase in stations from 1961 as well as long past the closing of the border in 1961. From this particular analysis we can only conclude that there was a line recorded in 1967 that had stations both in Kreuzberg and in East Berlin. 

This analysis, like the coverage analysis, suffers considerably under the limitations presented by sporadic station data. Here, too, the slight increase in stations reachable from the surveyed districts after 1951 may be majorly affected by changes in what stations were recorded in 1961 and later. It is also more difficult to interpret than the other visualizations as it relies on comparing colors between different map regions. With these limitations, and its rather ambiguous results, it can only be said to very tentatively support an improvement in connectivity after 1951, and disprove any major differences in connectivity between the two districts. 

### Modes of Transit (Pie Chart) Analysis:

This analysis shows the proportion of transit types among lines for each year and each district. There are four transit types represented in our sample years: bus, tram, u-bahn, s-bahn. We included this analysis as a kind of overview, to get an idea of what the transit system would have looked like. While it does give an idea of what public transit options a person would have had available to them, this analysis cannot be directly interpreted as a measure of connectivity. However, it does have merit as a descriptive analysis. This analysis could have been alternatively visualized using a segmented bar graph, or even a plot of “percent of lines” for each transit type. However, given its current application as an overview, pie charts are an easily interpretable visualization.

The pie charts show a similar trend across both districts, namely one of de-diversification. Both districts phase out trams, Kreuzberg in 1976 and Wilmersdorf in 1967. Additionally, S-Bahn lines were reduced significantly to none in Wilmersdorf and just one in Kreuzberg by 1984. When comparing the charts from 1951 and 1984 directly, it is apparent that buses overwhelmingly increase in proportion to the whole, moreso than the U-Bahn lines do. The pie charts cannot tell us whether the removed trams and s-bahns were replaced directly by other modes of transport running the same route, however we can interpret that in terms of proportions, bus lines replaced trams and s-bahn lines. 

This analysis is particularly limited by it not being quantifiable as either positive or negative in relation to connectivity. As such it cannot be used directly to support either part of the thesis, though it does describe the two districts’ transportation networks as similar.
